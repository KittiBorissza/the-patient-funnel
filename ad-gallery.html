How to read this gallery

Eight concepts, four stages, one genuine test

These are independent paid-social concepts, written as part of my application, not as work Invity has asked for or signed off. They start from the same three ideas as the homepage and the Content Calendar: a real first purchase, questions people ask, and a typo explained kindly. Nothing here is a price prediction, a promise of returns, or a reason to hurry.

I used Invity’s public newsletter editions as research, not as a script. The April, July, August and September 2026 issues, and the 17 August 2026 bear-market article, are the ones I actually read, on 10 November 2026. Where an angle comes from one of those issues, the card says so. I have not invented newsletter copy, and I have not described a proposed audience as one Invity already runs.

Remarketing and in-app event audiences are proposed setups. They would depend on consent, on platform eligibility, on campaign rules, and on whether the data is available. Product details, including fees, custody, withdrawals, Auto Send and Auto Buy, are from public pages and those newsletters, and they stay pending verification. Market-specific legal claims stay pending too.

No campaign images were supplied with the brief or in the repository, so I have not reused any. The three stills on this page are typographic mock-ups in this site’s colours, labelled as independent concepts. I would not invent an app interface. Walkthrough covers should use an approved recording once one exists; until then the typographic still stands in.

After someone engages

If someone wants more, let them choose

Watching a video or reading a post is not the same as asking for a sequence of ads, or subscribing to a newsletter. If someone wants more, let them choose: read the guide, ask a question, subscribe to the newsletter, or stop there.

The stages below are a way to think about the next useful message, not a path every person should be walked through. Remarketing audiences remain proposed setups, and they depend on consent, platform rules and the data you actually have.

The only A/B test

Same film, two hooks: fees or custody

The test already described on the homepage is the only one in this gallery that I would call a real A/B pair. The footage is the first-purchase walkthrough. To isolate the wording, the opening visual stays the same, and only the first line changes. If we also cut to a different first screen, I would treat that as a package test, not as a clean test of the words.

Fees-first

“This is the screen to actually read. What you’ll pay, before you tap anything.”

Custody-first

“Where is your bitcoin after you buy? With a custodian, separate from the company’s money.”

Measure first: watch time past the hook, watch time past the fee screen, and the questions people ask. If first purchases can be tied to tracked links, look at those second, per market. Never call a winner on a small sample, and not until the test has been repeated. A hook that wins attention and muddles the product is not the hook to keep.

The two cards are A first purchase, fees-first and The same first purchase, custody-first. The organic film is in the Content Calendar.

Funnel stage

Cold

Someone who already has a rough idea what bitcoin is, and has not spent time with Invity. The job is to be interesting and honest enough to earn a watch, not to force a download. Culture leads one idea; a practical first purchase leads the other, tested two ways.

The typo, for people who don’t yet know Invity

I AM HODLING

A typo from 18 December 2013, written while the price was falling. Today it means: I’m not trying to time this. The fine print: holding doesn’t protect you from losses.

Who it is for. People who are curious about Bitcoin language or history, and may not yet know Invity. This is a proposed targeting idea, not an audience Invity already runs.

What we want to learn. Whether people stay to the fine-print line, and whether they click the explainer. Purchases are the wrong main measure here.

Production notes

Audience signal. A proposed culture-led prospecting set: people interested in Bitcoin history, language, or a first plain explanation. This is a targeting idea, not a statement that Invity already runs this audience.

The hesitation. What does HODL actually mean, and is this just insider language I’m not in on?

Call to action. Watch the story

Destination. The 25-second film, or a simple page that carries the same script and a link to the original Bitcointalk thread. Not a hard install, and not a buy button.

Visual. Typographic 4:5 still: the words I AM HODLING, the date, and the fine print about losses. No meme merch, no rocket, no price. A finished independent mock-up is on this page. A motion version can be the first frames of the Reel.

Placement. Instagram and Facebook feed at 4:5, and Reels or Advantage+ placements that can take the same vertical film. LinkedIn only if the typo is told as a short history, not as slang for its own sake.

Main success metric. Hold rate to the fine-print line, and clicks to the explainer. Purchases are the wrong main measure at this stage.

Why this stage. Cold audiences need a reason to stop that does not depend on already trusting the app. The typo is famous enough to do that, and the fine print is how this account introduces itself.

A first purchase, fees-first

This is the screen to actually read.

What you’ll pay, before you tap anything. A first bitcoin purchase, every screen, including the fee line. Bitcoin can lose value. Make sure you understand the risks before deciding whether to buy.

Who it is for. People who know roughly what bitcoin is and have not engaged with Invity — a proposed cold audience, not an existing one. This is one half of a pair, not a test to run in every market at once.

What we want to learn. Whether a fees-first opening holds attention past the hook and the fee screen, and what questions people ask. First purchases from tracked links come second, and only once the sample can support a comparison.

Production notes

Audience signal. People who know roughly what bitcoin is and have not engaged with Invity. Broad interest prospecting, or a lookalike built later from viewers of the walkthrough — both are proposed, not existing Invity audiences.

The hesitation. What will it actually cost me to buy, and will I see that before I’m committed?

Call to action. Watch the walkthrough

Destination. The 35-second film, or a page that also says, plainly, who holds the bitcoin afterwards and that it can be withdrawn. The film should be able to stand alone if the click never happens.

Visual. Typographic 4:5 still with the headline, until an approved recording exists. When it does, a single still from the fee line — a real screen, never a reconstructed one — can replace the typography as the cover. A typographic mock-up is on this page.

Placement. Reels and Shorts first, with the same opening as a 4:5 feed still. This is half of the only genuine A/B test in the gallery.

Main success metric. Watch time past the hook, and past the fee screen, plus the questions people ask. First purchases from tracked links come second, per market, and only once the sample can support a comparison — never a winner on a small sample.

Why this stage. Fees are one practical question a first-time buyer may have. Leading with them is a way to be useful before anyone has a reason to trust the brand.

The same first purchase, custody-first

Where is your bitcoin after you buy?

Invity’s public pages describe a custodian, separate from the company’s money, and withdrawal to your own wallet. Those terms, including any limits, fees or conditions, need checking before this runs. The film still shows the fee line, and it still says that bitcoin can lose value.

Who it is for. The same proposed cold audience as the fees-first film. The two ads should run as a pair, not as two unrelated ideas, and not in every market at once.

What we want to learn. The same measures, read as a pair. A hook that holds attention but leaves people confused is not the one to keep.

Production notes

Audience signal. The same proposed cold audience as the fees-first film. The two ads should run as a pair, not as two unrelated ideas, and not in every market at once.

The hesitation. Once I buy, where is the bitcoin, and is it mixed with the company’s money?

Call to action. Watch the walkthrough

Destination. The same film and the same page as the fees-first ad, so the opening visual stays the same and only the first line changes. If the first screen also changes, treat it as a package test.

Visual. The same typographic system, with this headline in place of the fee line. Do not change the colour, the end card, or the footage after the opening. When an approved recording exists, the cover can be a real custody or withdrawal screen, never a mock interface.

Placement. The same placements as the fees-first ad, split evenly, same dates, same markets.

Main success metric. The same measures, read as a pair. A hook that holds attention but leaves people confused about fees or custody is not the one to keep, even if it is ‘ahead’ on watch time.

Why this stage. This is the other half of the test already described on the homepage. If both hooks are not running against the same film, it is not a test, it is two campaigns.

Funnel stage

Engaged

Someone who watched, saved or clicked an explainer. The next hesitation is usually about getting bitcoin out, or about who holds it in the meantime. Audiences built from those actions are a proposed setup. They would depend on consent, on what the platforms allow, and on whether the data is actually there. I am not describing an audience Invity already runs.

Can I move my bitcoin out?

Can I move my bitcoin out of Invity?

Invity’s public pages say you can withdraw to a hardware wallet or any Bitcoin address you control. That needs checking for current limits, fees and conditions. Moving it comes with a miner fee. Check the address twice, and send a small test first if the wallet is new.

Who it is for. People who watched, saved or clicked a first explainer and have not made a first purchase — a proposed remarketing set, only if consent, platform rules and the data allow it.

What we want to learn. Whether the “can I leave?” answer gets saved or clicked through to the guide.

Production notes

Audience signal. A proposed remarketing set: people who watched, saved or clicked the walkthrough or the HODL explainer, and have not made a first purchase. Building that set depends on consent, platform rules and the data you actually have.

The hesitation. If I buy, can I get the bitcoin out, or is it stuck in the app?

Call to action. See how withdrawal works

Destination. The six-slide carousel, or Invity’s public withdrawal guide. Auto Send can be a later slide, attributed to the August 2026 newsletter, not the hook.

Visual. Typographic 4:5 still that is the question, then the word Yes. A finished independent mock-up is on this page. App screens only if they are approved recordings.

Placement. Feed at 4:5, and Stories or Reels cut from the same carousel. Frequency capped, because these people have already given us some attention.

Main success metric. Saves and clicks to the guide. If withdrawals or onboarding continued among this group can be measured with consent, look at those second, on a small sample, and never as a guarantee the ad ‘caused’ them.

Why this stage. After a first explainer, a useful next question to investigate is whether people still want to know if they can leave. Answering that is how an engaged person may decide whether a purchase is safe to try.

Who holds it in the meantime

Held with a custodian, separate from the company’s money.

After you buy, Invity says your bitcoin is held with BitGo, a regulated custodian, not mixed with the company’s money. Withdrawal is described as available on the public pages, and should be checked for current limits, fees and conditions before this runs.

Who it is for. The same proposed engaged set, especially people who watched the walkthrough but dropped around the custody line.

What we want to learn. Whether clicks to the custody page lead to more specific questions than “is this safe?”

Production notes

Audience signal. The same proposed engaged set, especially people who watched the walkthrough but dropped around the custody line, or who clicked and did not continue.

The hesitation. Who is holding my bitcoin after I buy, and is it separate from the company’s money?

Call to action. Read how custody works

Destination. Invity’s public custody explainer, or a short film that does not skip withdrawals or the line about losses.

Visual. A typographic still, or a simple approved diagram, not a fake dashboard and not a reconstructed app screen. On-image type should be the headline and, in smaller letters, “You can withdraw it any time.”

Placement. Feed at 4:5, and in-stream or Reel placements if the film exists. Do not run this and the withdrawal question as two competing hooks to the same person in the same week if frequency is already tight; they are complementary, not a test.

Main success metric. Clicks to the custody page, and whether the questions that come back are more specific than “is this safe?”

Why this stage. Fees and withdrawals do not cover the trust question on their own. This is the fuller custody answer, for someone who already cared enough to watch.

Funnel stage

High intent

Someone who has looked at the product, or started in the app, without completing a first purchase. If app events can be used, the message can meet them at the confirm screen. If those events are not available, the same message still has to work as an ordinary ad. Both versions are below.

The confirm screen, if we can reach people there

Before you confirm, read three things.

The amount, the fee, and where the bitcoin will be held. There is no prize for tapping quickly. Bitcoin can lose value. Make sure you understand the risks before deciding whether to buy.

Who it is for. People who opened the app, started onboarding, or reached a confirm screen without a first transaction — a proposed event-based set that depends on consent and whether those events can be used in ads.

What we want to learn. Whether people who see this complete a first purchase, compared with a holdout if one is possible, and never as a winner on a small sample.

Production notes

Audience signal. A proposed event-based set: people who opened the app, started onboarding, or reached a confirm screen without a first transaction. This depends on consent, on whether those events are available and allowed in ads, and on platform eligibility. It is a setup to ask for, not an audience I am claiming already exists.

The hesitation. I’m at the confirm screen, or close to it, and I’m hesitating.

Call to action. See the three checks

Destination. A short checklist carousel, or the walkthrough starting at the confirm screen. Not a countdown, and not a ‘complete your purchase’ push.

Visual. Typographic checklist on 4:5: Amount. Fee. Where it is held. If an approved confirm-screen recording exists, it can sit under the type. Never invent that screen.

Placement. Feed and Stories, low frequency, limited date range, so it does not follow someone around after they have decided not to buy.

Main success metric. Completed first purchases among people who saw the ad, compared with a holdout if a holdout is possible. If it isn’t, use return-to-app and completed first purchases, and never call a winner on a small sample. Click-through rate is not enough.

Why this stage. At this stage the person already found the product. The useful message is how to read the last screen, not another introduction to bitcoin.

The same three checks, with no event targeting

Before you confirm, read three things.

If you’re about to buy bitcoin for the first time, stop on the confirm screen. Read the amount, the fee, and where the bitcoin will be held. There is no prize for tapping quickly.

Who it is for. Anyone we can reach without in-app events, because event-based audiences are often unavailable.

Production notes

Audience signal. Anyone we can reach without in-app events: people who visited the site or the app store listing, or simply the same first-purchase creative mix. This version exists because event-based audiences are often unavailable, or not yet allowed.

The hesitation. If I’m about to buy bitcoin for the first time, what should I read before I confirm?

Call to action. See the three checks

Destination. The same checklist or the same point in the walkthrough as the event-based ad, so the message is identical even if the targeting is not.

Visual. The same typographic checklist. Do not add ‘we noticed you started a purchase’ language, because this version cannot know that.

Placement. In the same feed and Reel mix as the cold walkthrough, or as the last frames of that film. It can also live on the landing page, where no audience targeting is required.

Main success metric. Scroll or watch-through to the three checks, and first purchases from the shared tracked link, knowing this cannot be isolated as cleanly as the event-based version. The point is whether the message still works when we cannot be clever about who sees it.

Why this stage. A high-intent idea that only works with a perfect event audience is fragile. This is the version I would run if all we had was the film and a page.

Funnel stage

After a first purchase

Someone who has already bought. Auto Buy is a later decision, not a reason to buy the first time. A newsletter or in-app note is the right first home for it. Paid social is only a fallback.

Auto Buy, said to people who already bought

Auto Buy, without the sales pitch.

You choose an amount and a rhythm. It does not time the market, it does not promise a better price than buying once, and it does not protect you from losses. If you would rather buy once, that is fine.

Who it is for. People who have completed a first purchase. Talk to them in the newsletter or the app first; paid remarketing is only a proposed fallback, and only with consent.

What we want to learn. Auto Buy set-ups among people who saw the note. An honest “this isn’t for me” is a useful result, not a failure.

Production notes

Audience signal. People who have completed a first purchase. The first place to speak to them is the newsletter or the app. A purchasers remarketing audience for paid social is only a proposed fallback, and would depend on consent, platform rules and whether that audience can be built at all. I am not describing an audience Invity already runs.

The hesitation. Should I set up a routine now, and what would I be signing up for?

Call to action. Read what it does, and what it doesn’t

Destination. The does-and-doesn’t carousel, preferably inside a newsletter issue or an in-app story. A paid landing page only if the owned channel cannot carry it.

Visual. Typographic 4:5, two columns or two slides: What it does. What it doesn’t. No upward chart, no ‘set and forget’ smile, no forecast. On-image type should include the line about losses.

Placement. Newsletter and in-app first. Paid feed or Stories only as a limited test, only to people who already purchased, and only if that is allowed. This is the wrong message to put in front of cold buyers as a reason to download the app.

Main success metric. Auto Buy set-ups among purchasers who saw the note. An honest “this isn’t for me” reply is a useful result, not a failure. If a paid test runs, click-through rate is not the main measure.

Why this stage. Recurring buying is a second decision. It should not follow someone who has never bought, and it should not be shouted at someone who has just bought as if the first purchase were incomplete.

If these go into production

Production briefs for the new stills

All three finished mock-ups on this page follow the same brief in miniature: 1080 × 1350, this site’s purple, paper, ink and amber, typographic rather than illustrated, and clearly labelled “Independent concept — not an Invity ad.” No coins, no rockets, no price charts, no mock interface.

HODL still, for the cold typo ad

On-image type, in this order: “I AM HODLING.” Then, smaller, “A typo, dated 18 December 2013.” Then the fine print, which must stay readable at phone size: “Holding doesn’t protect you from losses.” Background in the deep purple of this site, amber underline under the main words, paper-coloured type. Leave generous margins. The independent-concept label sits at the foot, in small caps, and stays on any recut.

Withdrawal still, for the engaged question

On a paper background, the question in dark type: “Can I move my bitcoin out of Invity?” Then, in the purple, the answer: “Yes. Any time.” A third line, smaller: “To a wallet you control.” No screenshot of a send flow unless it is an approved recording of the live app. The same independent-concept label at the foot.

Fees-hook still, for the first-purchase film

Deep purple background. On-image type: “This is the screen to actually read.” Then, smaller: “What you’ll pay, before you tap anything.” No reconstructed confirm screen and no invented fee figure. When an approved recording exists, a single real still of the fee line may replace this cover; until then typography is the honest choice. Same independent-concept label.

For any walkthrough or confirm-screen ad, the moving image has to be an approved or supplied recording of a real purchase. I have not made one, and I would not stand a designed ‘app’ up in its place. Local versions should be recut in the language the app actually shows in that market, once that is confirmed.

Research, not a script

What the newsletters actually gave this gallery

I read these public editions on 10 November 2026, on the Invity blog:

  • April 2026 newsletter. Useful background on cost basis and on Bitcoin being always open. Neither became a paid hook. Cost basis belongs with existing holders, in a newsletter, not in a cold ad.
  • July 2026 newsletter. Bank iD as a Czech-only verification route, which belongs in a local recut of the walkthrough, not in a generic ad.
  • August 2026 newsletter. Auto Send, including why a set amount avoids tiny withdrawals, informs a later slide on the engaged withdrawal card, attributed, with market-specific figures pending verification.
  • September 2026 newsletter. Daily frequency for Auto Buy, pending a live-app check, may appear in the post-purchase explanation. I did not use moving averages, a golden cross, Turbo Buy, or card cashback as paid angles.
  • Bear market, don’t panic, 17 August 2026. The line about worries being natural is used in the organic calendar, not as a paid “buy the dip” ad. I have not used the forecasts in that article.

May and June 2026 newsletter posts were not read through as editions, so no card is built from their body copy. Ideas that are not tied to an issue above — including the fees-versus-custody hook test and the typo explainer — are my own hypotheses, drawn from the homepage and the strategy page.
