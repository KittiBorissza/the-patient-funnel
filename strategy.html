      Experimental sample size

      How large each group needs to be for a controlled A/B test

      Two-proportion test, 80% power, 5% two-sided: n per group ≈ (1.96 + 0.84)² × (p₁(1 − p₁) + p₂(1 − p₂)) ÷ (p₁ − p₂)², adjusted for an uneven split. This is how many impressions or link clicks each group must reach before a difference means something. It is not the same as the delivery volume above, and Meta does not split delivery evenly unless you use its A/B test tool or separate ad sets.

      How the forecast works. The test at the top starts from one market and one monthly Meta budget. Impressions = budget ÷ CPM × 1,000; link clicks = impressions × CTR; implied CPC = budget ÷ clicks. People who watch past the opening = impressions × the illustrative hook-hold rate. First purchases = budget ÷ cost per first purchase (direct mode, €60 by default), or clicks × click-to-install × install-to-first-purchase (funnel mode). When the budget funds more than one group, it is split by the ad-set shares, evenly by default; that even split is an assumption, not how Meta always delivers. “Budget needed” keeps two different things apart: the Meta delivery volume (how many first purchases a budget buys) and the experimental sample size (how many people each group needs before a difference means something). The full monthly plan below the test uses the synthetic conversion model: cost per install rises as a channel spends more (base × (1 + 0.15 × spend ÷ €1,000) ÷ scenario factor); first buyers = installs × buy rate, with the scenario factor (Cautious 0.7, Base 1, Upside 1.3) also scaling the buy rate, capped at 100%. Referrals are capped per market; referral money beyond that is reported as unused. Search, creators, referrals and community stay on synthetic cost-per-install figures, because a single honest CPM/CTR standard does not apply to them in the same way. Media and rewards only: no salaries or production. A low spend per first buyer doesn’t prove profit, or that the buyers wouldn’t have come anyway.

      Where the industry figures come from. Country CPMs are 2026 estimates from AdSpendSpy for a standard Meta campaign (Facebook and Instagram). They are model estimates, not quotes from Meta. The European direct-to-consumer medians — €12.76 CPM and a 1.19% link CTR, with the middle half at €9.19–€16.56 and 0.92%–1.48% — are from adtribute’s Meta Ads benchmarks for September 2026, from a panel of European e-commerce brands. That is not a Bitcoin or regulated-finance panel; a product like Invity can cost more in the same auction, which is why the planning CPM can be typed over. I do not have a published CTR for each country, so the same European median is the default in every market until Invity’s data replaces it. The country CPM and the European DTC CTR come from different sources: used together they can imply a cheaper click than adtribute’s median CPC of €1.06. If you want to plan for a more competitive auction, raise the CPM toward the DTC median. Adjust’s finance app report (in Assumptions) is quoted in US dollars as comparison context only. The €60 cost per first purchase and the hook-hold, click-to-install and install-to-first-purchase rates are illustrative, not published benchmarks and not Invity data.

      What I’d test

      Seven creatives, each with one question to answer

      Each has a hypothesis and one metric. I would write the work so the same test can be compared across markets, with a local language version in each one. A native speaker would check every local version, Invity’s compliance would check claims, and ad approvals would be checked per platform and market. The forecast is how I’d see where we have the volume and the review capacity to run a comparison — not a promise to switch every market on at once. Hungarian is the one language I can check myself.

      Showing the fee and custody screens makes people more likely to start sign-up than a general product ad.

      First 30 days

      The first month

      • Weeks 1–2: talk to support, product and the people who know each market; read the comments in every language we can.
      • Build a multi-market forecast we can measure against — with CPM, CTR and language versions in one view — and replace the industry defaults with Invity’s account data as soon as it is available.
      • Agree what we want to learn from one useful comparison, and set up measurement so the same test can later be read market by market.
      • Make and review that comparison in the first market where the forecast shows enough volume, with a native-language check and a compliance check. Then decide where to adapt it.
      • End of month: share what we learned, what the sample can and cannot tell us, and which markets are ready for a language version next.
